\chapter{Evaluation}
\label{chap:Evaluation}

Throughout this thesis, we have used the automotive domain as the basis for illustrative examples and to motivate the design of \tool\ and its features. This is because, within the automotive domain, we can identify two main issues that this methodology addresses: how to handle variants within a product family and maintain traceability across it. For automotive manufacturers, there are variants in the customer-facing product, evident in the different vehicles offered, the model-year differences between vehicles, and the various trims available within a single vehicle model. There are also variants that can exist internally to the manufacturer as they go through development and decompose the vehicle into its components: engine, transmission, cooling, and heating variants that are used across platforms, for example. Managing these product families across multiple levels of abstraction requires product family traceability to track the requirements constraints that drive the various design and implementation decisions. 

For the evaluation portion of this thesis, we look towards the medical domain and medical device development. There is significant overlap between medical device development and automotive manufacturing, as both involve safety-critical systems that must comply with various standards and regulations across geographic regions to sell their products to customers. The medical device we will be using for this evaluation is a pacemaker. From a safety-critical perspective, medical devices such as pacemakers tend to have a greater impact on individual health and safety, which is often reflected in medical regulations. Regional legislation may affect product development, and the products themselves may have variants tailored to the capabilities of target stakeholders, in this case, individual patients. As this research has been carried out in Canada, we reference medical device guidelines and classifications based on Health Canada regulations~\cite{CanadaGuidanceDocument, VerifyDevices} as we are from a Canadian institution. According to these guidelines, an implantable rate-responsive pacemaker is classified as a class 4 medical device. This is the most critical classification a medical device can receive, as it is considered a closed-loop system used to control the treatment of a patient's condition. Thus, a pacemaker has high safety-critical implications. This justifies high levels of scrutiny for the requirements and potential variations, as there must be a great deal of confidence in the product lifecycle before being implanted in a patient.

This evaluation will use an existing requirement document from Boston Scientific~\ref{apdx:Req} for pacemaker development. We will assess how this methodology and \tool\ can be used to establish traceability between features and requirements. We will also consider potential integration points with development processes to evaluate the adaptability of the methodology and \tool\ and to minimize disruption to existing documentation and development strategies. We may also observe as part of this methodology opportunities to improve the requirements, though this is not a primary evaluation criteria.

This evaluation aims to show feasibility and applicability over a full empirical evaluation. Ultimately, to fit within the scope of a thesis, this is a proof-of-concept. Industrial and user evaluation, scalability testing, and quantitative analysis against existing technologies were out of scope for this thesis.

The first step in this thesis is to examine how domain analysis is conducted. This part of the evaluation examines how we can enhance existing knowledge-capture approaches by introducing domain analysis techniques from our methodology. Using the proposed processes, along with \tool, we will assess the ability to identify gaps in the original pacemaker requirement document, potential integration points within the existing development process, and general enhancements to engineering practices.

We also want to identify what a pacemaker is. As previously identified in accordance with Canadian guidelines~\cite{CanadaGuidanceDocument, VerifyDevices} and the Boston Scientific requirements document~\ref{apdx:Req}, we have an implantable rate-responsive pacemaker. The purpose of this pacemaker is to treat various forms of cardiac arrhythmia, or abnormal heart rhythms. There are three types of arrhythmia:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Arrhythmia:} A condition wherein the heart beats irregularly or abnormally
	\item \textbf{Bradycardia Arrhythmia:} A condition one can have is a Resting Heart Rate (RHR) that is abnormally slow. The question of “how slow is abnormally slow?” may differ from person to person because the heart’s RHR tends to decrease with age and in individuals with higher fitness levels. For most adults, a slow RHR is less than 50 or 60 Beats per Minute (BPM). Symptoms of bradycardia may include fatigue, dizziness, fainting, heart palpitations and breathing problems.
	\item \textbf{Tachycardia Arrhythmia:} A condition wherein one has an RHR that is abnormally fast. For most adults, a fast RHR is above 100 BPM.
\end{itemize}

A pacemaker system typically comprises two main subsystems: the pulse generator and the leads. The pulse generator, also known as the ``PG", contains the battery of the pacemaker and the circuits that deliver the pacing stimuli. It provides protection for the pacemaker's internal circuits and the patient's heart from each other. There is a connector from the pulse generator to the leads subsystem. The leads are insulated wires that carry electrical stimuli from the pulse generator to the heart. They are the first component of the pacemaker to be implanted during surgery, usually via the subclavian vein. The electrodes at the end of the lead are attached to the heart.

Finally, as this is an implantable closed-loop system, a Device Controller-Monitor system is paired with the pacemaker. This device is used by physicians, nurses, and technicians to interrogate and alter the pacemaker's behaviour according to patient needs. It provides a means of remotely transmitting programming data to the pacemaker while it is implanted and of receiving telemetry data from the pacemaker.

A more in-depth description of what a pacemaker is can be found in the appendix~\ref{apdx:What_Is_A_Pacemaker}. The document provides greater detail on the Cardiac Conductive System and its interaction with the pacemaker.

\subsection{Goal Diagrams}

The full requirement set by Boston Scientific for a pacemaker~\cite{scientific2007pacemaker} is available in the Appendix~\ref{apdx:Req}. Though not explicitly stated as domain analysis, knowledge capture occurs in chapters 1 and 2 of the pacemaker requirements, which are all captured in natural language. In fact, all the requirements specified by Boston Scientific are captured in natural language. These two chapters introduce the scope of the requirements, identify some stakeholders, and define the system and subsystems. We found it sufficient for engineers to have an idea of why the system is needed, who they are building it for, and where it is intended to be used. This comes from anecdotal accounts from industry engineers. They want to be able to justify their time and effort in building a product that has a purpose, even if they do not have all the details of the finished product. We now attempt to improve this domain analysis using the goal diagram and \gls{UCD} approaches outlined in our proposed methodology.

The first step we took in this domain analysis was to provide more information about what happens at each stage of the pacemaker lifecycle.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Pre-Implant:} This stage is where the patient is assessed to determine if a pacemaker is required for treatment. This stage includes risk/benefit discussion, patient risk tolerance, and informed patient consent. The patient has the right to refuse care and may deny getting a pacemaker altogether. It may also be determined that, though the patient may need a pacemaker, it would not be in their best interest to have it implanted.
	\item \textbf{Implant:} This stage is the surgery. The patient undergoes surgery to implant the pacemaker.
	\item \textbf{Follow-up:} This stage occurs directly after the pacemaker is implanted. The patient is assessed for surgical safety (correct pacemaker implantation, no infection, no other surgical complications, etc.). This is the follow-up assessment to ensure the surgery was successful and to identify any unforeseen complications after the surgery is complete.
	\item \textbf{Ambulatory:} This stage is the regular day-to-day life of the patient post-surgery. This includes regular check-ups with the patient's medical team. Regular pacemaker assessment is performed at this stage. If necessary, pacemaker maintenance or updates may be required. In extreme or unusual cases, pacemaker removal may be required or refused.
	\item \textbf{Explant:} This stage is where a pacemaker is removed from the patient. A cost-benefit analysis is performed with the patient to determine whether removing the pacemaker is more beneficial than leaving it in. Also, the pacemaker has to be removed after a patient's death.
	\item \textbf{Disposal:} This stage is when a pacemaker has reached the end of its life and is safely disposed of.
\end{enumerate}

These definitions are helpful to engineers to get a better understanding of the complete product lifecycle after development. It helps inform the goals of the various stakeholders, providing insight into why the requirements are specified as they are. Further, these additional definitions helped us identify additional stakeholders beyond those in the original specification. \subsection{Use Case Diagrams}

The next step is to identify the use cases for each of these stakeholders and compare them with the uses identified in the original requirement document. We have identified a variety of tasks (from the goal diagrams) for the stakeholders, which loosely break down into two categories: tasks that require the pacemaker and those that are human-supplemented. According to the methodology and our primary objective of developing the product, we want to identify the use cases for which stakeholders will need the pacemaker system. These use cases should also be traceable to one or more goals for the stakeholders. This is critical to establishing end-to-end traceability for the entire product lifecycle. For our most immediate task, the traceability helps to justify the identified use cases based on stakeholder goals.

\begin{figure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pacemaker_device.png}
	\caption{Image of a pacemaker pulse generator on the left. Diagram of a pacemaker device with leads. Extracted from the supplement document `What Is A Pacemaker' found in \ref{apdx:What_Is_A_Pacemaker}.}
	\label{fig:pacemaker_device}
\end{figure}

In Chapter 2: System Definition of the pacemaker requirements document, the components comprising the pacemaker system are defined: the pacemaker device (also known as the pulse generator or PG), the Device Controller-Monitor and associated software, and the device leads implanted in the patient. Those three components become the boundaries of our UCDs. This is an easy integration point between our methodology and the existing documentation. We can save some rework by reusing the system and sub-system decompositions from the original document. The document does not explicitly state the features or use cases for each device, but there are overview sections we can use as a starting point for determining the use cases.

The PG device overview provides the following information~\cite{scientific2007pacemaker} summarized from the PACEMAKER document found in~\ref{apdx:Req}:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Monitor and regulate patient heart rate.
	\item Detect and provide therapy for bradycardia conditions.
	\item Provide programmable, single- and dual-chamber, rate-adaptive pacing, both permanent and temporary.
	\item In adaptive rate modes, an accelerometer is used to measure the physical activity resulting in a sensor-indicated rate for pacing the heart.
	\item Programming and interrogation via bi-directional telemetry from the DCM. Allows the physician to change the device's operating mode and parameters non-invasively after implantation.
	\item Provide the following historical data:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Sensor output data.
		\item Atrial and ventricular rate histograms.
	\end{itemize}
	\item In conjunction with DCM, provide diagnostic features including:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Real-time telemetry markers
		\item EGMs
		\item P and R wave measurements
		\item Lead impedance
		\item Battery status tests
	\end{itemize}
\end{itemize}

The DCM overview includes two subsystems: a hardware platform and pacemaker application software. It also outlines the DCM's role. The DCM is the primary implant, pre-discharge EP support, and follow-up device for the pacemaker system. It can be used in the operating room, the doctor's office, and the electrophysiology lab. The DCM communicates with the PG using its software and hardware sub-systems. The DCM overview provides the following information:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Program and interrogate a pacemaker.
	\item Command delivery of ``Pace-Now" pace.
	\item Acquire and show diagnostics (history) and lead signal measurement information.
	\item Acquire and show sensor history and trending information.
	\item Show visible and audible indications of communication between the DCM and PG device, including beeping and LED's for alerting the operator to error conditions.
	\item Acquire and show multi-channel monitoring including surface electrogardiogram and telemetered signals (e.g. EGM, annotated event markers).
	\item Print reports and strip charts.
	\item Monitor battery status.
	\item Output to external strip chart recorders.
	\item Manage windows for display of text and graphics.
	\item Set the date and time.
\end{itemize}

The lead system overview provides the following information:
\begin{itemize}
	\item The lead system implanted in the patient allows the device to sense the intrinsic electrical activity of the heart and deliver pacing therapy.
	\item The leads are connected to the PG via its header. All IS-1 bipolar leads are supported.
\end{itemize}

Based on the various overviews, we can begin creating the UCDs. A helpful insight the overviews provide that has been missed thus far is how the subsystems will interact with each other. Within the UCDs, we can model this by treating the various subsystems as stakeholders in the diagram environment. Based on the overview of the PG device, we can show the UCD in Figure~\ref{fig:pg_device_UCD_original_spec}. Three actors are explicitly outlined in the original specifications: the doctor, the patient, and the DCM. In this interpretation, we treated sub-bullet points as mandatory use cases of the original use case. This is shown in the `Provide diagnostic features' use case and its various included use cases. The same strategy is repeated for the `Provide historical data' use case.

In the original specification, the programmable pacing is listed within a single bullet point. However, when we attempted to model that point as a use case, we found it was highly overloaded. It inadvertently hid a lot of information behind a single use case. How many types of pacing are there? How many of the types of pacing need to be implemented and programmable in the pacemaker? How are the types of pacing related to one another? Does each type need to be implemented independently, or can we identify reusable portions of the system based on the requirements? By breaking it up and modelling it in a UCD, we explicitly state the information that was previously hidden in the specification. We can identify that both single- and dual-chamber pacing will be reused by the rate-adaptive part of the use case. Although not explicitly mentioned in this section of the original specification, it implies that the pacemaker should support both rate-adaptive and non-rate-adaptive pacing. This is further supported by the table on page 17 (see Figure~\ref{fig:Operating_modes}) of the original specification, which outlines the pacemaker's operating modes. 

We took a further liberty with the following `an accelerometer is used to measure the physical activity resulting in a sensor-indicated rate for pacing the heart' use case. Since it is still related to rate adaptive pacing, we model it as another included use case along the chain. Thus we can show how the doctor interacts with the `Provide programmable pacing' use case and all of its included use cases along the relationship chain. These connections are not obvious from the natural language requirements in the original document, but become much clearer when the UCD is introduced as part of the process. It forced us to consider who was interacting with the use cases and how the identified use cases might imply knowledge that could be beneficial for further requirement specification or design. We have identified a possibility for reusable artifacts extremely early in the development lifecycle. We have also refined our use cases with minimal overhead to the original document.

Next, we see in Figure~\ref{fig:DCM_UCD_original_spec} a possible interpretation of the information in the DCM overview in a UCD. While the original specification states that the DCM subsystem is further decomposed into a hardware platform and a software application, it does not distinguish between the two when specifying the use cases. In this initial modelling attempt, we try to define the use cases for the DCM with its components combined within the system boundary. We also discovered another actor during this UCD: the Strip Chart Recorder. This actor is related to the singleton use case `output to external strip-chart recorders'. This UCD was particularly challenging to create due to all the conjunctive sentences in the specified features. In particular, the `Acquire and show diagnostics (history) and lead signal measurement information' use case is difficult to fully capture who it is for. The language is confusing, as using the word diagnostics makes the statement seem like this feature is for the Technician. However, because it then specifies history in brackets, similar to what is specified in the `Acquire and show sensor history and trending information' use case, we ultimately decided it is more likely to be used by the doctor and nurse actors. Interestingly, likely due to the overlap between the two stakeholders, the doctor and nurse actors in this model are associated with all the same use cases. Given their overlapping goals, we have greater confidence in the model's correctness.

It is more challenging to have confidence in the UCD of the DCM system due to the combined nature of the hardware and software sub-systems specified in the requirements. They are coupled in section 2.2.2 of the PACEMAKER document, which makes it difficult to identify what a software use case is and what a hardware use case is, as they are currently written. More information may come to light during refinement; however, at this point in development, it is difficult to gain further insight into how the subsystems could be decoupled.

The leads subsystem is relatively straightforward to model given the limited use cases identified. It identified two actors that will use it: the patient and the PG device. However, we have included the DCM as an actor for this system as well. We justify this because the DCM is the interface that allows human actors, such as doctors or nurses, to program or interrogate the pacemaker. 

Overall, creating the UCDs for the pacemaker has given us insight that would have been overlooked if we had relied solely on the original specification. We have identified potentially reusable parts of the system in the rate adaptive pacing. We have identified additional actors that were otherwise implied or overlooked in the original specification. We have identified potential pain points due to high coupling among the DCM subsystems. Finally, we have identified interactions among the various components of the pacemaker system. We also noticed during this process that a lot of interpretation is required to create the models. This can introduce inconsistencies, as the results depend heavily on the engineers who execute this process. 

\section{Product Family Development}

\begin{figure}

	\label{fig:pacemaker_fm}

One of the benefits of this methodology becomes apparent at this stage of development. Leveraging the equivalency we established between use cases and features, all the use cases we used in our UCDs are also the features we will use in our feature models. In Figure~\ref{fig:pacemaker_fm} we show a potential product family based on the UCD for the \pgd. We say 'potential' because there is variability in how engineers interpret the information from the UCD and goal diagram to create the feature model. For example, single- and dual-chamber pacing appear twice within the product family because both rate-adaptive and non-rate-adaptive pacing are required. There is overlap between the two features, but we put the rate-adaptive pacing under that feature and the non-rate-adaptive pacing on a separate level of the model.

Based on the UCD for the \pgd, every use case is necessary for the system, and we identified the reuse of single- and dual-pacing across rate-adaptive modes. As a result, there is no potential for variability in the final product, since all features are mandatory for completing the system. This provides a starting point for developing the product. We do not take this model as complete, as we expect to learn more about the system through the requirement refinement process. As we refine the model, this presents opportunities for iteration between the phases of this approach. As more features are identified, there can be benefits in iteratively linking the feature model and \gls{UCD} to explore how they relate to the actors and other features in the system.

\subsection{Composition}

Composition is a key component of the modelling strategies for this methodology. It is through composition that we encapsulate feature requirements; each requirement is owned by a specific feature of the product. This prevents floating requirements that cannot be traced to a specific feature. It also implicitly forces each feature to be well-defined, at least based on the quality of the requirements it owns. The composition relationship between features and requirements in this methodology enables us to achieve feature-requirement traceability as an almost by-product of good engineering practice.

Due to the current metamodel definition of \tool, feature composition differs slightly. Since we state that all requirements are scoped by their owned features, we do not support cross-cutting requirements. When a feature composition is required to define a product based on the feature model, it becomes more of a checklist rather than more traditional composition weaving. This is by design, as we want each feature to be self-contained with respect to its requirements. This does not exclude dependencies between features, however. Feature dependency validation can still occur during the composition process if properly implemented. At this time, feature composition is not an automated process in \tool. It requires the user to manually select which features from the product family to add to a product variant they define. Despite being a manual process, the tool specification helps ensure that when a feature is added to a product, all owned requirements are added as well to obtain a valid result, thanks to the natural semantics of composition in SysML~\cite{sysml2019omg}.

\section{Requirement Engineering and Refinement}

With a product family derived from the UCD, we can start exploring use cases and refining them into more well-defined requirements. We do this by taking the use cases and refining them into user stories. A user story outlines the steps a user (a stakeholder) would need to take when using a product (a use case) to achieve a goal or task, as outlined in the goal diagrams. We use the following structure when refining a use case to a user story:

\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Use case}: Provide programmable pacing
	\item \textbf{Actor}: Doctor, DCM
	\item \textbf{Precondition}: Pacemaker \pgd\ is active and can connect to the DCM.
	\item \textbf{Trigger}: Doctor wants to program pacemaker to mitigate patient bradycardia symptoms via DCM interface.
	\item \textbf{Success Scenario}:
	\begin{enumerate}
		\item Doctor accesses \pgd\ via DCM.
		\item Doctor verifies they are connected to the correct \pgd.
		\item Doctor selects the desired pacing operation mode.
		\item Doctor sets the various parameters based on the chosen pacing operation mode (pace pulse characteristics, rate sensing settings, delays, etc).
		\item Doctor pushes programmed mode and parameters to the \pgd.
		\item Doctor disconnects DCM from \pgd.
		\item Doctor logs off from DCM.
	\end{enumerate}
	\item \textbf{Secondary Scenarios}:
	\begin{itemize}
		\item Doctor connects to the wrong \pgd\ via DCM.
		\item The doctor makes a mistake when programming the \pgd\ via DCM.
		\item Communication fails or is interrupted during push from DCM to \pgd.
		\item Doctor is unable to access the \pgd\ via DCM.
	\end{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Success Postcondition}: \pgd\ is correctly programmed and pacing the patient's heart correctly and safely.
\end{itemize}

The steps outlined in the success scenario are the functional requirements for the use case. They outline what the actor needs to do to accomplish the task. The outcomes in the secondary scenarios provide alternative results of the actor's actions. These alternative outcomes can be safety hazards, security hazards, system malfunctions, or user errors. It is important in this step to ensure that `what' the stakeholder has to do to achieve their goal is captured, not `how'.

The secondary scenarios outline the justifications for mitigating requirements to improve the chance that the actor will achieve the success postcondition, in this case that the \pgd\ is correctly programmed and that the patient's heart is paced correctly and safely. In the context of our methodology, mitigating requirements are most commonly categorized into safety, constraint, or qualitative requirements. At this time, we have not ruled out that a mitigating requirement may lead to a functional requirement; however, based on our experience, we support the convention that they are better suited to non-functional requirements. The secondary scenarios come together to answer the question of `how' the stakeholder will accomplish their objective, typically by creating requirements that reduce the likelihood of the stakeholder reaching a secondary scenario.

As we have a minimum viable product as part of this methodology, we can also compare it against other existing technologies. In summary, FeatureIDE is typically an implementation-focused feature-modelling tool. GEARS is a design-focused feature modelling technology that emphasizes capturing variation points in design. DOORS is a requirement database technology that supports manual creation of traceability links and can generate graphical representations of those links. The tool for \tool\ differs from these tools in that it integrates requirement-modelling with feature-modelling in a way none of the previously mentioned technologies currently do. Compared to DOORS, the traceability links are a first-class citizen of \tool, while in DOORS they are optional. Unlike GEARS and FeatureIDE, \tool\ attempts to capture requirements variation before moving to design or implementation details. It tries to capture changes in rationale that could lead to variations in how to solve a problem for a customer, which ultimately result in different feature and requirement variants. Overall, \tool\ and its implementation fill in a niche in development that does not conflict with other existing technologies. 

\section{Iterative and Incremental Change}

For the purposes of this methodology, iterative changes are understood as cycles between development phases (features and requirements, requirements and design, and design and implementation). The idea behind this definition is that lessons learned during a subsequent development phase can inform and justify repeating a development task from a previous phase. In this methodology, if a need or gap is identified during the requirements phase, it may necessitate repeating a process in the feature modelling phase. A need or gap between the requirement modelling and feature modelling phases could be that some requirements do not fit into any features. It may require a review to ensure that the identified features are complete and correct. 

Incremental changes are changes within a single development phase. We used this definition to limit the scope of what constitutes an incremental change. This was to support our development efforts for tool implementation and to simplify requirements for incremental changes compared to iterative changes. Currently, our tool implementation of \tool\ supports maintaining traceability matrices through incremental changes as defined. As engineers work through the steps of a development phase, they will continually add, modify, or remove artifacts until they reach a satisfactory result. We consider all of these incremental changes until they get enough completed to move on to the next phase of development.

Our implementation supports three traceability matrices for \tool: project traceability, feature-feature traceability, and feature-requirement traceability. Project traceability and feature-requirement traceability are defined to focus on traceability of the requirement entities. Project traceability is the matrix that tracks the entire project. Feature-requirement traceability is a traceability matrix that is scoped to a single feature. The Feature-feature traceability matrix represents the feature model that is defined within the project.

\begin{sidewaysfigure}
	\centering
	\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{pacemaker_feature_traceability.png}
	\caption{Feature-feature traceability during the middle of development for a pacemaker.}
	\label{fig:pacemaker_feature_traceability}
\end{sidewaysfigure}

These matrices are a second syntactic representation of the modelling environments present within the tool implementation. Since they are semantically equivalent, any change to the requirements in the requirement canvas or to the features in the feature modelling environment is automatically reflected in their respective traceability matrices. They are semantically equivalent because the traceability matrices are based on the same metamodel that is used to specify the modelling environments. As a result, the matrices are a complete reflection of the modelling environment. The first column of the matrix is type class, while everything within a row is directly traceable to the element of the type class in the first element of the row. For example, in figure~\ref{fig:pacemaker_traceabilit1}, the first column is of type \texttt{Requirement}, and all the elements within the same row are found based on the traceable relationships that are defined for the \texttt{Requirement} class from the metamodel. The relationships are specified for display in the table using queries from AQL~\cite{aql} on the user-created models. For this reason, for every model element that is added, to a requirement element, it will appear in one or more of the requirement traceability matrices.

Due to the shared source of the traceability matrices and the modelling environments, they are tightly coupled. Therefore, correctness of the matrices is completely bound to the correctness of the models created. If the relationships created between elements in the modelling environments are incorrect, then the traceability matrices will also reflect incorrect traces between elements. This reinforces one of the objectives of the \tool\ methodology which is that good engineering practice will lead to good traceability as a natural by-product. Further, by having two different viewpoints of the same elements and relationships, the tool provides different ways for the stakeholders to interact with the features and requirements to allow for better refinement of both over incremental changes.

The tabular environments can also define new requirements. The limitations are that test cases and reviewers cannot be created in the tabular environment. This is by design, as it forces users to manually create those traces explicitly in the modelling environment and reflect on what test cases are being created and who is approving the requirements.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:Eval-Discussion}

We currently have limited support for the defined iterative changes in the tool for \tool. While changes to an existing model are automatically reflected in the traceability matrices, their impact cannot be assessed in the tool at this time. Based on the metamodel for \tool, however, the mechanisms to support change impact analysis are all present. This is due to the model's inherent traceability and to the requirement that explicit connections between elements be present for a model to be considered syntactically valid under our current conventions. A more in-depth change-impact analysis that performs both syntactic and semantic analysis would be desirable to inspire greater confidence in the results.

The \tool\ implementation is also currently only scoped to feature and requirement modelling. It does not currently support \gls{UCD}s or goal diagrams. As a result, we do not automatically generate and maintain traceability to those phases of development. While this does pose a challenge to our goal of end-to-end traceability, we wanted to focus on the feature-requirement traceability and build more confidence in that portion, given the novelty of the feature-requirement encapsulation, before adding the goal-to-use case-to-feature-to-requirement traceability, as there is less novelty in that specification. There was also no other obvious connection point between goals, use cases, and features, as we found we could use features to encapsulate requirements. This lack of completion also hampers our efforts to support iterative changes across the methodology's steps and development phases. This also represents a limitation of the \tool\ methodology. Because we do not connect goal diagrams and use case diagrams with our other modelling specifications, we lose another benefit of this methodology: traceability as a by-product of good engineering practice. This is explored in more depth in the future work chapter, as we aim to determine how to effectively integrate these four modelling strategies into a single specification. By having all the modelling environments connected within \tool, we will have greater confidence in tracing iterative changes and assessing change impact more comprehensively across the various stages of development.

As part of this evaluation, we observed that following the \tool\ methodology led to improvements of the requirements on top of identifying the features and generating traceability. While not one of the main contributions of this thesis, it is important to note the bias present within this evaluation given our own intimate knowledge of all aspects of the \tool\ methodology. This is however somewhat mitigated by the incremental and iterative nature of this approach as the expectation is for the stakeholders to learn more about the processes and the product they are developing throughout the development lifecycle and refine all the engineering artifacts produced by \tool\ over time. \tool\ has demonstrated the potential to improve the feature modelling and requirement engineering stages of development by combining existing \gls{MDE} and \gls{PLE} techniques, but it it still highly dependent on the qualifications of the people executing the steps of the methodology

Overall, \tool\ shows potential for addressing gaps in current requirements engineering practices. The methodology provides strong support for traceability across the various stages of execution. Each step naturally flows into the next, and every subsequent step can be directly supported and traced to preceding work. Currently, there is a gap between the goal diagram, the \gls{UCD}, and the feature and requirement modelling environment due to a scope limitation in the initial specification. Despite this current limitation, we have shown the potential for traceability between the various modelling environments. The current challenge is finding the optimal mechanisms for connecting the various modelling environments.

Further, once all the modelling environments can be traced to each other mechanically, this will advance the goal of a more comprehensive change-impact analysis. It will also enable end-to-end traceability to support argumentation and justification of the specified product's safety and security, enhancing safety and security assurance. In this current iteration of \tool, we have demonstrated how to automate traceability between features and requirements. We have also justified our various classifications of requirements, in line with previous work, while further refining them to help with specific development goals for the product. We have shown how to flow from domain analysis through to requirement elicitation and how to justify each decision along the way.
