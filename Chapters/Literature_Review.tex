\chapter{Literature Review}
\label{chap:lit_rev}

There are four main research topics that support the work in this thesis: \gls{MDE}, \gls{PLE}, traceability, and requirement engineering. Individually, each of these topics has many branches of research, both academic and industrial. To narrow the selection of work to support this thesis, we sought papers that explored at least two of the four topics. As bonus topics, we also considered papers that involved a supporting tool or an exploration of adoption in industry. 

We also include some definitions in this chapter to establish nomenclature that will be used within the rest of this thesis. For \gls{PLE}, we consider a product line, a product family, and a feature model to be synonymous ways of capturing product information by connecting features. Similarly, we consider model-based system engineering and model-driven engineering as synonymous practices.

Traceability is scoped to requirement traceability and feature traceability, though not necessarily to each other. Much of the literature focuses on traceability itself, while a smaller body of work examines it across specific phases of development. As such, we seek prior work that explores a range of traceability methodologies and approaches. With these guiding topics, we use the following strings to generate our search results:

\section{Product Line Engineering}

\gls{PLE} has become a popular approach to handling diversity and complexity in a company's product portfolio since its original conception by Kang et al.~\cite{kang1990feature, kang2002feature}. We scoped the related work to fundamental aspects of \gls{PLE} and concrete implementations (tool support). As one of the objectives of this thesis is to add a few extensions to the existing theories of product families, we paid special attention to formal specifications and existing tools.

Many tools exist for feature modelling. Two examples of tools come in FeatureIDE and GEARS. FeatureIDE~\cite{kastner2009featureide, thum2014featureide}, developed originally by K\"{a}stner and Th{\"u}m et al, is a feature modelling plugin tool for Eclipse that lets a user define their product software as a feature model, write code within the model, and seamlessly integrate the features together to form an executable product. It has more recently become the research community standard as a feature modelling tool. Another example for feature modelling and PLE is GEARS~\cite{GEARS} by Big Lever, which emphasizes its 3-tiered software product line methodology. The 3-tiered approach outlines three ways to use GEARS: handling variation and automation within a single product line; identifying reusable assets across product lines; and product evolution over time. 

As a formal approach to \gls{PLE}, feature modelling can also be defined through algebraic methods as shown in the work of H\"{o}fner et al.~\cite{hofner2006feature,hofner2011algebra} which uses set theory as the basis for proving how their algebra works. This algebra can be extremely useful for tool development that implements feature modelling, as it enables the computation of feature models, thereby facilitating easier implementations of feature modelling and product line engineering tools.

Another direction that feature modelling has taken is evident in the work of Czarnecki et al.~\cite{czarnecki2004staged}. While still involving formalisms for developing feature models, there is a greater focus on graphical modelling rather than mathematical specification and computability. They expand on the original syntax defined in FODA and show, by using context-free grammar (in this case, metamodelling), how to add cardinality to feature models and their relevance to feature modelling paradigms. This specification's main benefit is handling feature duplication in contrast to the work of H\"{o}fner et al.

The similarity between feature modelling and \gls{UCD}s has been previously explored in the work of Mefteh et al.~\cite{mefteh2015implementation}. They implemented a rigorous approach to transforming use cases from a \gls{UCD} to a feature model and implemented it in a tool. The methodology proposed in this thesis instead relies on engineering judgement to extract the features from the \gls{UCD}. In the future work of this thesis, we do however want to explore more rigorous tool support for extracting features from use cases similar to what Mefteh et al. have done. Instead, the contribution of this thesis is the integration of the equivalency between use cases and features into the broader scope of feature modelling, requirement engineering, and automated traceability into a unified workflow.

We know that there is a desire for better \gls{PLE} practices for industry. In a survey paper by Horcas et al.~\cite{horcas2019software}, they conclude that a lack of tool support for the various approaches to \gls{PLE} is a roadblock to more widespread adoption in industry. This is further supported by the workshop conducted by Becker et al.~\cite{becker2024not} where they identified 7 themes to the challenges of \gls{PLE} across various industries: Modularity, Product Line Scoping, Variability Management, General Process, Verification and Validation, Evolution and Maintenance, and Key Performance Indicators. There is some ongoing work to address some of these issues. Bilic et al.~\cite{bilic2019integrated} present an \gls{MDE} toolchain and methodology for managing variability in system design. Co-evolution is another problem that Jongeling et al. ~\cite{jongeling2020co} address in Simulink models within a product line. For the automotive industry specifically, we can see some initial benefits from an implementation by Wozniak et al.~\cite{10.1145/2791060.2791071} that uses GEARS. Their act of capturing their product line in a consistent, model-based environment helped manage complexity. The ability to automate configuration calibrations was a great benefit for their purposes as well. As these were just their initial efforts, they also called for further research and work in this domain with respect to industry practices. These publications highlight that a gap exists between academic and industrial practices due to the difficulties of widespread adoption in an industrial setting.

\section{Requirement Engineering and Modelling}

We consider requirement engineering the act of eliciting, refining, and specifying requirements for a given item, whether a product, feature, or component. However, we identified a gap in the literature regarding \gls{MDE} techniques and requirement engineering, specifically in requirement modelling. This will be a significant contribution of this thesis as we explore this gap and identify how \gls{MDE} techniques can enhance existing requirement engineering approaches.

We also explore informal and formal approaches to requirement engineering. They both have merits in different situations, with informal methods being more approachable, while the formal methods inspire more confidence due to their natural rigour. We also found various definitions of functional and non-functional requirements that were helpful in informing our design decisions for this thesis.

%Requirement engineering is a critical portion of this research. Part of the contribution of this thesis is the advancement of requirement modelling. As such, we look for related work that has addressed combining \gls{MDE} with requirement engineering.

The idea of using feature models to encapsulate requirements was partially inspired by Meyer's book on requirement engineering~\cite {meyer2022handbook}. His style emphasizes extensive stakeholder analysis to determine their use cases and to scope system requirements around user interactions. It emphasizes a more informal approach to requirement engineering, lowering the bar for entry and making it more approachable, while recommending the occasional ``formal picnic" as he refers to it as needed. The purpose of the ``formal picnic" is to supplement requirement decisions and specifications with more rigorous methodologies as needed, rather than mandating formal methods be adhered to throughout the requirement engineering process.

We also have Lamsweerde's style of requirements~\cite{lamsweerde2009requirements}. There is a lot of overlap between Meyer's and Van Lamsweerde's requirements; however, there are some key differences in the ease of implementing the requirements processes. Van Lamsweerde's requirement style dives deeper into the formalism and MDE approaches to requirement engineering, in contrast to Meyer's requirement style. Lamsweerde used formal goal diagrams to capture knowledge about a product's stakeholders and to justify emerging requirements derived from the goals. Another difference is in the definition of a goal. Van Lamsweerde defines a goal as a ``prescriptive statement of intent that the system should satisfy through the cooperation of its agents." In contrast, a goal is defined by Meyer as the ``needs of the target organization, which the system will address". In general, Van Lamsweerde's style requirements are a more in-depth version of Meyer's requirements, with a more formal approach to requirement engineering, at the cost of Meyer's approachability.

%We focused on MDE requirement engineering techniques, feature modelling strategies and approaches, and other work related to traceability in product lines. To remain within the scope of requirement engineering, we focused on resources that emphasize general requirement engineering rather than specific requirement notations. 

Due to the gap we discovered in requirement modelling, we focused many of our efforts on other requirement engineering resources that did not include \gls{MDE} as a main component of their approach. Robertson~\cite{robertson2012mastering, robertson2000volere} heavily emphasizes stakeholder identification and involvement early on in the project. There is more emphasis on business use cases and functional vs. non-functional requirements, in contrast to Meyer's requirements, which focus more on stakeholder use cases. Meyer's requirements style is also structured into four' books,' each representing a different portion of the requirements engineering process, with a suggested structure for organizing its respective section. By contrast, Robertson's requirement engineering approach focuses more on how requirements are written and their specific categorizations. The requirement engineering process of Sawyer and Sommerville~\cite{sommerville1997requirements, sommerville1997viewpoints} discusses the different viewpoints within system requirements. Their work stresses that not all stakeholders are people; they can be anything from an organization to another system to be integrated with or interacted with. There are many more that could be talked about; however, we can generalize a pattern:
\begin{itemize}
	\item Identify who/what you are developing for.
	\item Identify what/how the stakeholder interacts with a target system.
	\item Specify the behaviour around that interaction.
\end{itemize}

While requirement diagrams from SysML~\cite{sysml2019omg} are a helpful approach for requirements engineering and modelling, other modelling schemes have also been explored in this area. Use case diagrams from UML~\cite{fowler2018uml} have been widely explored for their value in requirements engineering. Siau and Lee ultimately concluded that use case diagrams helped communicate requirements compared to other modelling approaches~\cite{siau2004use}, but had limited usability beyond communication. They have also been used by von der Ma{\ss}en and Lichter~\cite{von2002modeling} for software product line development, having to customize the metamodel to make it work. Wegmann and Genilloud~\cite{wegmann2000role} formalized use cases and use case diagrams for functional requirements in systems, though they faced difficulty scaling the model to handle more complex systems in detail and demonstrated limitations of use case diagrams. FORML, a Feature Oriented Requirement Modelling Language by Atlee and colleagues~\cite{Beidu2019, 6345799}, is another approach to feature-scoped requirements based on product families. They use superimposed state machines to express the behavioural requirements of the features that compose a product line.

%Another related requirement engineering approach includes the work of Suzanne and James Robertson~\cite{robertson2012mastering, robertson2000volere}, which offers many helpful definitions of functional and non-functional requirements and their relationships to business cases, similar to Meyer's use cases. Pete Sawyer and Ian Sommerville~\cite{sommerville1997requirements, sommerville1997viewpoints} have another requirement engineering approach which discusses how system viewpoints affect the scope and definitions of requirements. Finally, Axel Van Lamsweerde's requirement engineering style~\cite{lamsweerde2009requirements} uses a similar approach to requirement engineering as Meyer's but has a much greater emphasis on the use of formal methods for requirement elicitation and specification. While useful for proving the requirement specification, this goes beyond the goal of this paper, as we focus primarily on the implementation of \tool\ and the methodology it supports.


\section{Traceability}

Traceability is a commonly studied topic in academia and industry. We found that traceability was commonly considered a retrospective task of product development due to the difficulty and cost of maintaining it throughout development. We found that this is due to a common philosophy that separates traceability as a task from the development process, rather than including it as a natural outcome of development. This has led to a variety of post-release analysis techniques to build traceability artifacts.

This thesis aims to embed traceability as a natural part of development rather than a retrospective task, and we explored various automated and manual approaches to traceability. We kept the scope of traceability artifacts broad to help explore traceability across development phases beyond the feature and requirement phases.


%For the scope of this thesis, we explore traceability as it is used in \gls{PLE} and \gls{MDE}. We further scope related work based on how traceability is used with requirements. 

The concept behind this thesis shares some similarities with the work of Marques et al.~\cite{6945504}. While both aim to automate traceability matrix generation and maintenance by using requirements diagrams, there are distinct differences in the implementations and end goals. We focused our efforts on distinct \gls{MDE} environments (requirement modelling, feature modelling, use case diagrams, and goal diagrams). Meanwhile, the approach from Marques et al. aims to generate traceability between requirements, design, and testing.

Dronology, the work of Cleland-Huang and colleagues, is a project that has produced comprehensive work around and including traceability. As part of their project, they explore traceability through the lens of safety assurance~\cite{Cleland-HuangBCSR07, AgrawalC23, DBLP:conf/splc/Cleland-HuangAI20, mirakhorli2011tracing}.

%They cover automation and tooling for drone development and use traceability artifacts as part of their safety assurance activities~\cite{Cleland-HuangBCSR07, AgrawalC23, DBLP:conf/splc/Cleland-HuangAI20, mirakhorli2011tracing}.

The work of Heisig et al~\cite{heisig2019generic} proposes a generic traceability metamodel for end-to-end traceability in software product lines. Their proposed method is designed to facilitate and enable comprehensive traceability throughout the development process from requirements to implementation across multiple MDE tools. As such, they developed a traceability metamodel to handle a variety of artifacts they anticipate will require traceability links. In contrast, our work does not focus on developing a traceability metamodel. Instead, we focus on mapping requirements to features and generating traceability from the mappings. Another example of traceability in product lines is in the work of Tsuchiya et al.~\cite{tsuchiya2013recovering}. Their work focuses on recovering lost traceability links due to incremental development. They propose a framework and implement a tool that shows potential in recovering lost traceability links due to natural software maintenance over time. This is relevant because it is another approach to maintaining traceability through incremental software changes. While they focus on recovering lost links, we attempt to update the links in parallel to changes.

%Heisig \textit{\textit{et al}}~\cite{heisig2019generic} focused on using a generic traceability metamodel for software product lines. They concluded that using a metamodel facilitated the traceability of various artifacts throughout development. 

Similarly, the work of Kelleher~\cite{kelleher2005reusable} also required the definition of a traceability metamodel, though it was developed to handle complexity in software systems rather than product lines. Another approach can be found in the work of Maletic et al.~\cite{maletic2005xml}, whose primary focus was the flexibility and interoperability of traceability artifacts, enabling the direct modelling of artifacts through model transformations using XML. Asuncion et al.~\cite{asuncion2010software} used machine learning to generate traceability prospectively and retrospectively, during development and after development. It ultimately opened the possibility of continued research but did not draw any final conclusions on the effectiveness of using ML to generate prospective traceability.

There has also been previous work on the development of tools to support traceability in feature modelling and \gls{PLE}. The work of Anquetil et al.~\cite{anquetil2008model} they build a tool based on a generic traceability metamodel that can be applied across multiple different design artifacts. On key difference between their work and the work presented in this thesis is that they assume several key steps have already been completed: requirements engineering, variability management, UML design, and implementation. In contrast, we are specifically targeting requirements engineering and variability management as the key artifacts that need to be developed. UML design and implementation as out of scope for this thesis. Further, Anquetil et al. apply traceability onto those artifacts after they have been created, while we aim to generate traceability directly as part of the development process. 

The work of Sousa et al.~\cite{sousa2008model} is also similar to the work of Anquetil et al. as they also create a generic traceability metamodel to be applied to a broad spectrum of engineering artifacts to discover and document traceability links. The key difference between these previous works and our own is that we do not have a generic metamodel to be applied after engineering artifacts have been created. Instead, we want traceability to be a fundamental part of creating the artifacts in the first place. This is a major contribution of this thesis as the majority of traceability effort takes place after the engineering artifacts have already been created, instead of integrated with the initial development. This will be further expanded upon in chapters~\ref{chap:methodology} and~\ref{chap:Implementation}


\section{Discussion}

Having reviewed a variety of publications on \gls{MDE}, \gls{PLE}, requirement engineering, and traceability, we have identified several patterns. While there are some \gls{MDE} techniques that support both formal and informal requirement engineering, there is a lack of literature on modelling requirements themselves. Specifically, the use of SysML requirement diagrams is an underexplored theme compared to more popular modelling strategies, such as \gls{UCD}s and, to a lesser extent, goal diagrams. 

There are many different strategies for handling traceability, from machine learning to manual processes. We also found prior work on generating traceability for a software product line. We found that we share a similar philosophy to Asuncion et al., who argue that traceability should be a prospective task rather than a retrospective one. While we share that philosophy, we approach it very differently, as we do not use machine learning in our traceability generation. We consider traceability should be a natural outcome of good engineering, emerging naturally as various phases of development are completed.

We generally agree with Meyer's approach to requirement engineering, which supports a more informal and flexible approach to requirements, with some detours into more rigorous methods to supplement and support requirement specification as needed. This is the main reason we follow a development path similar to the one he presents in his handbook.

Finally, \gls{PLE} and the industry require more support for the state of the practice. We have identified a desire for better \gls{PLE} across multiple industries, along with the many difficulties they face in implementing it. Although we do not aim to solve all of them in this thesis, we do aim to lower the bar for entry into implementing \gls{PLE} techniques in industry and to create tools to support these efforts.

The work presented in this thesis expands upon our previous work~\cite{10.1145/3652620.3687816, 10.1145/3652620.3688349} where we first introduce the concept of the feature-requirement hierarchy for traceability and the tool to implement automated traceability during the development of feature models and requirements. This has come together to form \tool, the methodology and its tool we will be presenting in this thesis.
