\chapter{Introduction}
\label{chap:Introduction}

What came first, the chicken or the egg? It is a question people often debate over dinner or on the bus. In engineering, there is a similar debate: what comes first, the feature or the requirement? Very often, when we do engineering work, we consider what we want to build in parallel to actually building it; exploring the problem space is often overlooked in favour of exploring the solution space. As a result, an engineer may have to reflect on their requirements when they hit a wall during development. They may find that their requirements were incomplete for their feature. Perhaps the scope of their feature is much larger than they anticipated; what was originally considered a single feature could in fact be multiple features. Thus, the engineer will iterate back and forth between feature and requirement development, incrementally changing each until they reach a system state they consider complete, or at least a minimum viable product. 

How do we capture this process? \gls{FDD}~\cite{palmer2001practical} is a method for agile development in which engineers identify features to be built together to create a system, often leveraging \gls{MDE} techniques. They then identify acceptance criteria, or descriptions of each feature, to specify what needs to be done and how to know when it is complete. These descriptions serve a similar role to requirements for the features, if not written as requirements for the features themselves. Therefore, we can make the proposition that before we have requirements, we have features. Features come first; we should focus on identifying them before we start thinking about the requirements for a given system. This situation will often arise when a new product is being developed or a company is just starting up.

There is an unusual assumption in this scenario; are there no existing requirements before we begin identifying features? From another perspective, in more mature development teams and industries, it is uncommon to start from scratch without any requirements, and even rarer to jump right into identifying solutions without first understanding the problems. Thus, even if limited, there are at least some guiding requirements before engineers begin to identify features of their system that will solve their problems. Therefore, we can say that requirements come before the features. In this environment, we already have a rich history to draw on to inform any new developments.

In reality, both scenarios are likely to happen, perhaps even within the same company. Innovation in industry is constant, and new products are constantly being developed, both from scratch and iteratively on existing products. We may be able to leverage existing assets from one product to help develop an entirely new one. Conversely, we may be branching off into an entirely new domain with no existing documentation or reusable assets. Thus, we have to start from scratch to understand the problems in the new domain. Both scenarios are equally valid!

The conflicting nature of reality generates a couple of problems for development. As a result, the relationship between requirements and features becomes ambiguous. In fact, what are the differentiating factors between a feature and a requirement? For this thesis, we will follow the definition of a feature from the original work of Kuang et al.~\cite{kang1990feature, kang1998form} for their Feature-Oriented Domain Analysis \gls{FODA} and Feature-Oriented Reuse Method \gls{FORM}. According to their original definition, a feature is "a prominent or distinctive user-visible aspect, quality, or characteristic of a software system or systems." Based on this definition, we could consider any portion of a system that a user can interface with a viable candidate for a feature. In fact, for the sake of inclusion, we extend this definition not only to be user-visible but also to serve as an interface, taking into account the multiple senses a user can use to interact with our system. This will expand the scope of a feature beyond visual aspects to include sound, touch, smell, and taste. Depending on the product scope, this definition could be extended to other subsystems that interface with the targeted product.

What is a requirement? This is a well-developed and studied topic. To answer, we look to van Lamsweerde~\cite{lamsweerde2009requirements}, who outlines the purpose of requirements engineering as answering three questions: what, why, and who? What is the problem we are trying to solve? Why is it worth solving? Who should be involved in the solution? There are many more requirements engineering approaches and strategies that we can and will use to develop our methodology. As a baseline, a requirement should answer the problem we are solving, supported by sound reasoning for why it is worth solving and who is involved in solving it. 

As such, what are the problems we are trying to solve with this thesis? For one, what is the relationship between features and requirements? This is a problem because of ambiguity in how requirements and features are specified, because they can (but do not have to) be defined at different levels of abstraction, and because they are defined and manipulated using different development methods, eg, agile, FDD, PLE. Clarifying these ambiguities by concretely defining the relationship between features and requirements may help ensure we elicit and refine requirements that are correct and reasonably complete. At the same time, it can help to ensure that the features we identify are correct and reasonably complete. We define this relationship in the metamodel found in the Methodology chapter~\ref{chap:methodology}.

Another issue is that iterations between features and requirements do not happen in isolation. There is usually a lot of supporting documentation and software that surround and depend on both the requirements and the identified features. This is usually documented through traceability, most commonly in a traceability matrix. However, maintaining a traceability matrix for all incremental changes between requirements and features is extremely tedious and time-consuming. For each change, it is usually a low return for a high-effort task, until the traceability matrix is so out of sync with both the requirements and features that it is no longer usable. One implication of this is that we will need to create an entirely new traceability matrix, and thus the cycle continues. Therefore, traceability documentation is a significant challenge during iterative and incremental development.

%\gls{FDD} as a development style misses a very key component. How do all the features fit together? This problem is addressed by Product Line Engineering \gls{PLE}. 

We also examine implications for agile development styles. \gls{FDD} is a feature-focused development methodology that already has established implementation rules. We want to explore possible adoption into existing development approaches. The objective here would be to explore methods for improvement rather than redefining \gls{FDD} entirely, especially by tying it in with \gls{MDE} and \gls{PLE} efforts. This is true at both the conceptual and tool-support levels. One such tool is FeatureIDE~\cite{kastner2009featureide, thum2014featureide}, which supports feature composition of software artifacts. Or a tool like GEARS~\cite{GEARS}, which emphasizes its 3-tiered software product line methodology. These tools are further discussed in the literature review chapter~\ref {chap:lit_rev}

There is also formal methods research for \gls{PLE} such as the work of Peter H\"{o}fner et al.~\cite{hofner2006feature,hofner2011algebra}, which created a formal algebra for how to compute product lines and feature models. What these tools lack, however, is a self-contained way to manage traceability between features and requirements; FeatureIDE focuses on code composition, while GEARS is not cohesive. GEARS requires hooking into separate requirements engineering tools and lacks a way to handle requirements independently. Thus, we identify another problem: a lack of tool support for traceability and iteration between requirements and features.

Finally, the problem is how we accomplish all of these tasks. What is the process to ensure the features we identify are correct and sufficiently complete? What is the process to make sure that we identify requirements that are correct and sufficiently complete? How can we be sure that our mappings between requirements and features also make sense? This is another problem, as we want to ensure that the mappings between features and requirements are correct and complete for both development and assurance purposes. We want developers to be developing the correct features, and we want to be able to reason about those features so we can build assurance that the properties of our system are true.

%A confounding variable in all of these problems that we have not yet identified \gls{PLE} is usually a \gls{MDE} process

It is important to note that while not mandatory, many \gls{PLE} efforts also use modelling in their approach. There is already significant cross-pollination between the two domains. For our purposes, we want to examine these problems primarily through the lens of \gls{MDE}; can we solve them using \gls{MDE} techniques and processes? We support \gls{PLE} through \gls{MDE} techniques and processes.

In summary, these problems lead to the following research questions:

	% \item[RQ2: ]\label{RQ:2} How can we improve traceability maintenance between features and requirements?
	% \item[RQ3: ]\label{RQ:3} How can we improve tool support for iterative and incremental development between features and requirements?
	% \item[RQ4: ]\label{RQ:4} How can we leverage \gls{MDE} techniques for domain analysis, problem space exploration, and requirement
	\item \label{RQ1} How can we improve the maintenance and generation of traceability between features and requirements?
	\item \label{RQ2} How can we improve tool support for iterative and incremental development between features and requirements?
	\item \label{RQ3} How can we combine \gls{MDE} techniques for domain analysis, problem space exploration, feature development, and requirement development?
	\item \label{RQ4} How can we improve the state of implementation and usability of \gls{FDD}?
\end{enumerate}


\section{Motivation}

In many industries, we can find companies with product catalogues that they offer to customers. Many industries also focus on developing safety-critical applications. Some areas where these two categories overlap include the automotive and medical device domains. In the automotive industry, companies offer a range of vehicles for customers to choose from. Further, each vehicle model on offer may have variants available. These vehicle model variations can be due to aesthetic differences or functional differences. There can also be variations depending on where the vehicle is sold, such as the driver's seat location, depending on whether the country drives on the left or right side of the road. 

Further, vehicles are inherently dangerous products. According to the Canadian government, 91533 vehicle collisions were reported in 2022, resulting in 1931 fatalities~\cite{CanadaCrashStats}. It is well documented that automotive manufacturing is a safety-critical industry, and automotive companies are constantly innovating and evolving to improve vehicle safety and reduce accidents, fatalities, and injuries. Thus, we can see that the automotive industry can benefit from \gls{PLE} to help manage the product catalogue, support documentation for developing safety cases (or assurance cases in general), and support iterative and incremental development as they release new vehicles year after year.

In medical device development, product variants are common, as companies may be required to fine-tune or adapt a product to different country regulations. Another point of variation can come from the intended use case of a medical device. The same device may be used to treat different diseases or ailments under different conditions, and enabling or disabling this kind of functionality can be critical for regulatory approval. It can also be part of the discovery process, as a new application for existing technology can be discovered. This event occurs frequently in medicine and medical technologies. The ability to plan out new product variants can be extremely beneficial for product planning and development.

Throughout this thesis, we will be using examples from the automotive domain. These examples aim to provide context for the work and aid in evaluating the methodology and tooling. Then, we use an example pacemaker project in the evaluation section of this thesis to examine the proposed methodology alongside an existing requirement document for a medical device.

\section{Hypothesis}

With the research questions defined and the motivation outlined, we can start making some guesses about how to answer them. Beginning with \ref{RQ1}, there are several modelling techniques to use. For domain analysis and the identification of system features, we propose using the \gls{UCD} from SysML~\cite{sysml2019omg}. As a modelling technique, it is informal; however, it is also easy to use for analysis and problem space exploration. The biggest reason to use \gls{UCD}s is the need to identify the system's actors/stakeholders and how they might use it. The idea of a use case is very similar to that of a system feature, allowing for a relatively easy mapping between system use cases and features.

Another \gls{MDE} technique we use for domain analysis and problem space exploration is Goal Diagrams as outlined in Van Lamsweerde's requirements engineering textbook~\cite{lamsweerde2009requirements}. Lamsweerde provides formal semantics for goal diagrams, which helps ensure we can properly reason about the system's goals and the users' goals. However, in spite of the formal semantics Lamsweerde has prescribed to goal diagrams, there are variant syntaxes that exist for goal diagrams that do not strictly adhere to the syntax and thus the semantics that Lamsweerde has defined. This has pros and cons. As a benefit, the reduction in formality can lower the barrier to entry, making it easier to use across professions as a back-of-the-envelope form of expression. The loss of semantics makes it difficult to use formal validation goals and the possible requirements that they can be used to derive. Given that we are more concerned at this stage with capturing knowledge, we are not concerned about the loss of formality. We found the increased flexibility more valuable, as it improves the usability of goal diagrams and their use for exploring the problem space to elicit requirements.

The reason we chose these two \gls{MDE} techniques is part of the answer to \ref{RQ3}. For the rest, we turn to the Handbook of Requirements and Business Analysis by Meyer~\cite{meyer2022handbook}. Meyer outlines several steps towards the requirement elicitation process, which also apply to feature identification. Parts of his book outline the importance of identifying the goals of both the system and the users, as well as user stories and use cases. Further, he supports the use of both formal and informal methods, leaving it to the engineer to decide when to use one over the other based on context. We support that notion, and much of the methodology we outline is inspired by his book. The \gls{UCD}s created for analysis will also be used to outline user stories to refine and justify the identified features. The goal diagrams from van Lamsweerde's requirements engineering are used to support the Goals book from Meyer in either a formal or informal capacity at the engineer's discretion.

To further explore \ref{RQ1}, we propose the following hierarchy: features shall encapsulate requirements. The reason for this proposal is two-fold. For one, in \gls{FDD} we often list requirements as scoped by a feature. This presented a history supporting the concept of a feature containing requirements. As \gls{FDD} is a common approach to development, we felt this would ease the conceptual learning curve of the proposed hierarchy and its implications. By encapsulating requirements within features, we can achieve feature-scoped requirement traceability, as each requirement is owned by a single feature. We will henceforth refer to this as feature-requirement traceability. And for the second point, there is more existing research and development on \gls{PLE} in an \gls{MDE} environment than on requirement modelling with an emphasis on traceability in an \gls{MDE} context.

The defined relationship between features and requirements also helps with  \ref{RQ2}. By formalizing the relationship between features and requirements, tool development becomes streamlined, as we can leverage existing Object-Oriented techniques to develop a \gls{DSL} that supports the iterative and incremental development of both features and requirements. We can leverage a self-contained tool to partially automate traceability maintenance when changing features or requirements in a feature or requirement model.

Finally, combining all the previous research questions, we arrive at the feasibility question \ref{RQ4}. By combining automated traceability with \gls{MDE} techniques and tool support for incremental and iterative development, an ideal outcome would be to enhance current \gls{FDD} implementations. 

In summary, we propose that features encapsulate requirements for supporting traceability. Leveraging this definition, we can build a tool that partially automates feature-requirement traceability. We can use existing \gls{MDE} techniques in \gls{UCD}s and goal diagrams for domain and problem space analysis, inspired by Bertrand Meyer's approach to requirements engineering.
